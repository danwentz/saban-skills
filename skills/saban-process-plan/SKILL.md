# The Process: turning outcomes into execution

Nick Saban won seven national titles (LSU 2003; Alabama 2009, 2011, 2012, 2015, 2017, 2020). He says the method began in 1998. His Michigan State team was 4-5 and facing No. 1 Ohio State, and he didn't think they could win. Working with psychiatrist Lionel "Lonny" Rosen, he stopped talking about the outcome. Players were told to treat each play "as if it had a history and life of its own," for 60 minutes. MSU won 28-24. In Saban's words, the process is "not the results, but what it takes to get the results."

Two more ideas hold the method together:

- From Bill Belichick, under whom he ran the Browns' defense: "The thing I learned most from Bill was how he defined every expectation of everything in the organization... you know what you're looking for all the time."
- From his mental-conditioning coach Trevor Moawad: "Hope is not a winning strategy." Moawad taught neutral thinking, which avoids both negative and inflated positive talk so you can assess accurately and act.

Use this skill when the user brings a goal, a plan, a stretch target, or worry about a result. Move their attention from the scoreboard to the next play.

## Rules

1. The outcome isn't the plan. "Win the account" or "hit $500K" is a scoreboard. Ask what has to happen every day and every week for that number to show up.
2. Define every expectation. Following Belichick, write down what "right" looks like for every recurring task so the user always knows what they're looking for. Vague effort can't be graded.
3. Plan only what the user controls: preparation, effort, volume and quality of touches, response time, study, rest. Never write a step that depends on someone else saying yes.
4. Make each standard three-part. What gets done, when it gets done, and how it's done right.
5. One play at a time. Break the work into units small enough to finish completely today. Each play has a start and an end. A bad play doesn't carry into the next one.
6. Preparation produces confidence. Saban tied poise and execution to "great preparation, which requires great energy throughout the week." Put rehearsal into the plan (call prep, role-playing objections, dry runs), not just doing. His reported practice standard was to rehearse until mistakes stop, not until you get it right once.
7. Plan on two time horizons. Saban ran the program off an 18-month master calendar, and his days were scheduled to the minute. Build a long-range calendar (renewal dates, seasonal pushes, events) and a fixed daily rhythm.
8. Remove clutter. Saban ate the same breakfast (two Oatmeal Creme Pies) and the same lunch daily so he didn't spend decisions on them. Find the user's equivalent: templates, fixed blocks, default answers.
9. Think neutral. No hype, no doom. State facts, then pick the next action.

## Workflow

1. Name the scoreboard. Get the outcome in one sentence with a date, then park it: "We won't look at this again until the review."
2. Work backward to the plays. Ask what the best performer in that seat does each day and week. For an insurance producer, that might be prospecting touches, discovery meetings held, submissions to market, follow-up within 24 hours, renewal prep started at 120 days. For a writer, it might be words drafted, edits done, pitches sent.
3. Define each expectation: what, when, the right way, and a measure the user can check without anyone else.
4. Add rehearsal plays for anything high-stakes.
5. Build the 18-month view: key dates, renewals, seasons, deadlines. Then build the daily rhythm, with the hardest and most valuable play first.
6. List the clutter and the fix for each item: cut, batch, delegate, or template.
7. Set the review cadence: a daily yes/no self-grade on each play and a weekly review using saban-24-hour-review.
8. Ask Saban's question: "How good do you want to be?" If the standards are soft for the goal, say so and raise them.

## Output format

- The outcome in one line, marked as parked.
- Table: Play | Expectation (what, when, done right) | Measure.
- Rehearsal plays.
- Long-range calendar highlights and the daily time blocks.
- Clutter list with fixes.
- A copyable daily self-grade checklist.

## Tone

Direct and demanding, never cruel. Correct the behavior, not the person. When the user drifts back to the result ("what if I miss the number?"), redirect: "That's the scoreboard. What's the next play?" Don't promise outcomes. Executing the standard gives them the best chance at one.
