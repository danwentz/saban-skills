# Building the roster: who you let in decides how good you get

Saban's dynasty ran on recruiting with a list. For each position, his staff set "critical factors" based on the system Alabama would run. Prospects were judged against those factors, not their reputation. "I do not care how many stars he has got," he said. The question was always why Alabama was recruiting this player. He compared recruiting without a plan to going shopping without a list. The habit traces back to Belichick, who "defined every expectation of everything in the organization" so that "you know what you're looking for all the time."

A few other features of how he handled people:

- Character was checked through other people, by talking to those who knew the prospect, because a short face-to-face meeting is an unreliable read.
- Coaches often joined in junior roles first. Steve Sarkisian, fired as USC's head coach, spent time at Alabama as a "fly on the wall" and an analyst before becoming offensive coordinator. That gave Saban a look at fit before trust, and gave people second chances, which earned loyalty.
- Leadership was a duty, not a status. A player leadership council relayed information and dealt with problems among teammates.
- There was one standard. Asked on 60 Minutes in 2013 why he was so hard on players, he said: "Mediocre people don't like high achievers, and high achievers don't like mediocre people." Starters and walk-ons practiced under the same demands.
- Demanding didn't mean cold. Sarkisian called him "one of the more compassionate human beings I've ever been associated with." Saban treated players' behavioral problems as something to manage, like a medical issue, rather than something to punish and forget.

Use this skill when the user is hiring, choosing prospects or clients to pursue, picking partners or vendors, building a team, or dealing with someone below the standard.

## Rules

1. Shop with a list. Before looking at anyone, define three to six critical factors for this seat or segment, drawn from the system they'll operate in. Each factor has to be observable.
2. Ignore the stars. Logo size, a referral source's excitement, a polished interview, a big premium: none of these count unless they're on the list on purpose. Keep asking why you're recruiting this one.
3. Check character through others. Talk to people who've worked with them, references, past vendors, how they treat staff. One meeting isn't enough evidence.
4. Character and work habits are must-haves, not tiebreakers. One person or account that lowers the standard costs more than it produces.
5. Look where others aren't looking. People coming off a setback with something to prove, overlooked segments, accounts bigger shops ignore.
6. Try before you trust. Use a junior role, a pilot project, a small first policy or a trial period to see fit before committing fully.
7. Recruit every day. The pipeline of people and prospects needs a recurring play in the plan (see saban-process-plan).
8. One standard for everybody. If the top performer gets exceptions, the standard is gone.
9. Develop with care, then decide. Give clear feedback tied to the standard, the support to fix it and a timeline. If the gap stays, move on.

## Workflow

### Selecting (hiring, prospects, clients, partners)

1. Ask what the seat or segment is, what system they'll operate in, and what winning looks like.
2. Draft the critical factors with the user: factor, why it predicts success in this system, how to observe it, and whether it's a must-have.
3. Weight the factors.
4. Grade each candidate 1 to 5 per factor with one line of evidence. Mark missing evidence as "unknown" and list who could supply it (the character check).
5. Rank them. Recommend against anyone who fails a must-have, whatever their total.
6. Propose a try-before-trust step and the next recruiting play for each top candidate.

### Holding the standard (existing team or book of business)

1. Restate the standard in concrete terms.
2. Compare actual behavior to it with specifics.
3. Draft the conversation: the gap, the expectation, the support offered, the timeline.
4. Set the check-in date and what "fixed" looks like.
5. If the user leads a team, suggest giving peer leaders real duties, along the lines of a leadership council.

## Output format

- Critical factors table: Factor | Why it matters here | How to observe | Must-have? | Weight.
- Grade sheet: Candidate | score and evidence per factor | weighted total | must-have pass/fail | unknowns to check.
- Recommendation in two or three sentences, with the honest reason.
- Try-before-trust step and next plays.

## Tone

Fair, exact and humane. Say no to a bad fit plainly. Be specific about the gap between where someone is and the standard, and make it clear you want them to close it.
