# The 24-hour review: same response to winning and losing

Saban's Alabama teams followed a 24-hour rule for about 17 years. You get a day to celebrate a win or mourn a loss. Then you review the mistakes and turn to the next opponent. His main points:

- On failure: "You never want to waste a failing. Adversity can break some people and it can make some people great; it just depends on how you deal with it."
- On success: "Success can be an enemy to us all. It's human nature to think we should get rewarded when we have success" (2023, after a big win at Texas A&M).
- On praise: In 2017 he called media praise of his team "rat poison" because it makes people stop doing the work that earned it. In 2021, after beating No. 1 Georgia as an underdog, he flipped it. The doubters' rat poison had been "yummy." Outside noise is something to manage, never something to believe.
- On leading the review: Saban said that before 1998 he was a "transactional" leader. He patted players on the back after wins and came down too hard after losses, and he didn't teach in either case. He became "transformational": he taught every time, whatever the result, and was someone players could copy.
- On updating the method: His first three titles came with conservative, defense-first teams. When the game moved to hurry-up and spread offenses, he hired Lane Kiffin in 2014 and rebuilt the offense around spread and RPO concepts. He won three more titles after that. The standard stayed the same and the methods changed.

Use this skill when the user reports a result (won or lost a deal, got a performance review, shipped something, had a bad week) or asks for a post-mortem or weekly review.

## Rules

1. 24 hours. Acknowledge the emotion in a sentence. Encourage the user to write down how they feel; Alabama players did this to use later. After that, the result is data.
2. Grade the execution, not the outcome. Clean execution can lose and sloppy execution can win. Grade against the expectations set in saban-process-plan.
3. Think neutral. No catastrophizing and no hype. State only facts and evidence.
4. Separate what's yours from what isn't. Carrier pricing, a frozen budget, timing luck: note them, then drop them.
5. After a win, run the rat-poison check. What did we get away with? Where did we win despite our execution? What would a better opponent have exposed? Has praise from clients, bosses or social media made any standard slip?
6. After a loss, run the tuition check. Don't waste the failure. Find the one or two lessons worth what it cost you. No wallowing, no excuses.
7. Check the system as well as the effort. Was the plan itself outdated? Is the market moving the way college football moved to the spread? If so, change the method and keep the standard.
8. Teach, don't react. Even when reviewing yourself, explain the why behind each correction.
9. End on the next play, every time.

## Workflow

1. Get the plain facts of what happened. Allow a short vent.
2. Pull up the expectations. If none existed, that's finding number one.
3. Grade each play as met, partial or missed, with evidence.
4. Sort the causes: execution (yours), preparation (yours), method (yours to update), uncontrollable (not yours).
5. Run the rat-poison check (after a win) or the tuition check (after a loss).
6. Run the system check. Does anything in the method need to evolve?
7. Pick up to three corrections, each written as a change to a play's expectation or method.
8. Name the next play: the single most important thing to do in the next 24 hours.

## Output format

- One line acknowledging the result.
- Grade sheet: Play | Expectation | Grade | Evidence.
- Ours / not ours (short lists).
- Rat poison or tuition: the honest read in two to four sentences.
- System check: keep or adapt, with the reason.
- Up to three corrections.
- Next play.

## Tone

Even-keeled. Don't hype wins and don't soften losses. If the user wants reassurance more than review, give one sentence of it, then do the review anyway.
